### "Drawing the Boundaries of Democracy: Partisanship, Anger, and the Public's Definition of Democracy." (First Author; with Frank J. Gonzalez)

<span style="display: inline-block;
             background-color: #E0F2FE;
             color: #0369A1;
             padding: 4px 10px;
             border-radius: 5px;
             font-size: 0.8em;
             font-weight: 500;">
  Under Review
</span>

<details style="margin-top: 12px;">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
Although Americans tend to tolerate violations of specific democratic norms when it favors their party, they often disagree about what constitutes democracy in the first place. In a preregistered online experiment, we investigate how     definitions of democracy are shaped by partisan anger. We consider the multidimensionality of democracy definitions, while paying particular attention to the understudied dimension of economic distribution as a possible component of democracy. We find that Democrats' definitions of democracy are broad, inclusive, and stable across emotional primes, whereas Republicans' definitions are affected by anger in unexpected ways. Specifically, strong Republicans are more likely to include economic distribution in their definitions of democracy when prompted with anger, and text analyses suggest that this is associated with concerns about economic hardship. We consider the implications of these findings, particularly with regard to normative questions about the role of emotions and partisan motivated reasoning in democratic resilience.
  </p>
</details>

### "Mobilized or Demobilized? Affective Polarization and Repertoires of Political Participation." (Solo-Authored)

<span style="display: inline-block;
             background-color: #E0F2FE;
             color: #0369A1;
             padding: 4px 10px;
             border-radius: 5px;
             font-size: 0.8em;
             font-weight: 500;">
  Under Review
</span>

<details style="margin-top: 12px;">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
Does affective polarization mobilize or demobilize political participation? Existing research generally finds that affective polarization promotes political participation, but this conclusion may obscure heterogeneity in how citizens participate in politics. Using the 2020 American National Election Studies, I employ latent class analysis to identify five distinct repertoires of political participation: Voters, Civic Activists, Donors, All-Round Activists, and Indifferents. Greater affective polarization is associated with higher probabilities of membership in Donors and All-Round Activists, but lower probabilities of membership in Voters and Civic Activists, while showing little relationship with membership in Indifferents. Further analyses reveal that in-party favoritism and out-party animosity play different roles across participation repertoires. These findings challenge a uniformly mobilizing account of affective polarization and demonstrate that understanding its implications for political participation requires attention not only to whether citizens participate, but also to how they participate.
  </p>
</details>

### "Partisan Asymmetries in Inhibition of Racial versus Partisan Prejudice." (First Author; with Alexandra McCoy, Lauren O'Rourke, and Frank J. Gonzalez)

<span style="display: inline-block;
             background-color: #E0F2FE;
             color: #0369A1;
             padding: 4px 10px;
             border-radius: 5px;
             font-size: 0.8em;
             font-weight: 500;">
  Under Review
</span>

<details style="margin-top: 12px;">
  <summary>Abstract</summary>
  <p>
An enduring yet untested claim in the affective polarization literature is that partisan prejudice is relatively untethered by anti-prejudice social norms compared to racial prejudice. In a U.S. national online survey experiment, we test how cognitive processing associated with prejudice inhibition affects racial versus partisan prejudice and how norms about race versus politics intersect. We find stronger partisan than racial bias, but partisan asymmetries exist in how social norms affect the two. Controlled processing decreases pro-inrace bias only among Democrats, whereas automatic processing increases pro-inparty bias among Democrats but decreases it among Republicans. Further, we find that partisan bias is reduced when race is salient among Democrats, but among Republicans, partisan biases are constrained by racial pro-white biases. These findings suggest the roles of social norm salience and prejudice inhibition vary across partisans and between racial and partisan prejudice. 
  </p>
</details>
